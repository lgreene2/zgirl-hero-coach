# Z-Girl Hero Within — Facilitator Orientation Guide

Purpose: prepare educators, coaches, youth workers, ministry leaders, program staff, and other approved facilitators to lead Z-Girl consistently, safely, and within role boundaries.

## Orientation standard

Every facilitator should complete this orientation before leading an institutional Z-Girl implementation.

Completion should confirm that the facilitator understands:

- the purpose of Z-Girl;
- the participant-choice model;
- the private-reflection boundary;
- the difference between facilitation and clinical / crisis / pastoral / medical roles;
- the approved institutional pathway/profile;
- accessibility expectations;
- the local safeguarding and emergency escalation route;
- what implementation information may be documented;
- how to respond when a participant does not want to continue.

## 1. What Z-Girl Is

Z-Girl Hero Within is a guided reflection and encouragement system. It helps participants pause, notice what is happening, identify strengths and support, consider perspective, and choose an achievable next step—a **Hero Move**.

Depending on the approved profile, Z-Girl may support:

- self-awareness;
- confidence and self-efficacy;
- character and values reflection;
- decision-making;
- leadership and personal development;
- athlete mindset and team reflection;
- trusted-adult connection;
- structured reflection within a school, youth, faith, or team setting.

## 2. What Z-Girl Is Not

Do not present Z-Girl as:

- therapy or counseling;
- psychiatric or psychological diagnosis;
- medical care;
- crisis intervention or emergency services;
- clergy or spiritual direction;
- sports medicine;
- a substitute for a qualified professional;
- a behavioral surveillance system;
- a tool for extracting private disclosures from participants.

A facilitator may hold another professional role, but when using Z-Girl, the facilitator should distinguish the Z-Girl reflection process from any separate professional service or legal duty.

## 3. The Facilitator’s Role

The facilitator is responsible for the **environment and process**, not for controlling what a participant privately reflects.

The facilitator should:

- introduce the purpose clearly;
- explain participant choice;
- use the approved profile and current materials;
- keep the pace appropriate to the group;
- support reasonable access and participation;
- avoid pressuring disclosure;
- notice implementation barriers;
- reinforce trusted-person and institutional support routes;
- follow local safeguarding/emergency procedures when required;
- document implementation issues without copying private reflection text.

The facilitator should not:

- demand to see private entries;
- require a participant to read a reflection aloud;
- interpret a private reflection as a diagnosis;
- create unofficial clinical or risk scores from reflection responses;
- promise secrecy that conflicts with legal or institutional duties;
- improvise a new faith, athlete, school, or specialized profile without approval;
- use Z-Girl to discipline, rank, punish, select, or publicly compare participants.

## 4. Participant Choice Script

Facilitators should communicate the following ideas in natural, age-appropriate language:

- You may pause if you need time.
- You may skip a prompt that does not feel right for you.
- You may stop the activity.
- You may ask for a trusted person.
- You do not have to read your private reflection aloud.
- If you need help beyond this activity, use the support people and procedures available in this setting.

The exact wording may be adapted for age, language, accessibility, and setting while preserving these choices.

## 5. Privacy Boundary

The standard institutional model is designed so that private participant reflection text is **not routine institutional reporting data**.

Facilitators should understand the difference between:

### Private reflection content

Examples:

- personal journal-style responses;
- private emotional descriptions;
- private statements about relationships or experiences;
- individual reflection narratives.

These are not collected for the standard institutional scorecard.

### Implementation information

Examples:

- whether a session occurred;
- approximate participation / opportunity to engage;
- technical barriers;
- accessibility issues;
- whether participants understood the process;
- aggregate usefulness / confidence / safety-respect responses where approved;
- facilitator observations about delivery quality that do not reproduce private reflection text.

These may be used for implementation learning according to the approved scorecard and agreement.

## 6. If a Participant Shares Something Concerning

Z-Girl does not replace the institution’s safeguarding, emergency, mandated-reporting, counseling, medical, pastoral, or other support procedures.

If a participant voluntarily shares information that triggers a facilitator’s professional, legal, or institutional duties:

1. remain calm and respectful;
2. do not turn the Z-Girl session into an investigation;
3. do not promise secrecy;
4. follow the institution’s approved escalation process;
5. contact emergency services or designated professionals when required by the institution, law, or immediate circumstances;
6. document only what the institution requires through its proper system—not in the Z-Girl aggregate scorecard;
7. do not place private disclosure details into routine Z-Girl implementation notes.

## 7. Accessibility & Inclusive Facilitation

A participant should not have to disclose a diagnosis to Z-Girl in order to receive reasonable implementation support.

Facilitators should be prepared to adapt, within the approved materials and local support structure:

- pacing;
- reading load;
- verbal vs. written access;
- screen size / device access;
- sensory environment;
- breaks;
- language support;
- facilitator-supported navigation;
- alternative response method where approved;
- group size or seating arrangement.

When specialized accommodations are needed, coordinate with the institution’s appropriate accessibility or support personnel.

Facilitator checklist:

- [ ] I know which Z-Girl pathway/profile I am using.
- [ ] I know the session sequence and timing.
- [ ] I have tested the relevant route/materials.
- [ ] I know the participant-choice language.
- [ ] I understand the private-reflection boundary.
- [ ] I know the local safeguarding/emergency contact or procedure.
- [ ] I know the accessibility support route.
- [ ] I know what aggregate measures are approved.
- [ ] I know what I should and should not place in implementation notes.
- [ ] I know who to contact for technical or implementation support.

## 9. Session Opening Pattern

A strong opening should normally include:

1. **Purpose** — why the group is doing the reflection.
2. **Choice** — pause, skip, stop, trusted-person option.
3. **Privacy** — private reflections are not being collected as routine institutional reporting.
4. **Boundaries** — Z-Girl is reflection and encouragement, not therapy or emergency help.
5. **Access** — explain how to request practical assistance.
6. **Start** — begin the approved reflection sequence without overexplaining or forcing disclosure.

## 10. During the Session

Facilitator behaviors to reinforce:

- allow silence and thinking time;
- avoid calling on participants to disclose private responses;
- normalize different levels of participation;
- redirect group discussion away from mocking, ranking, or pressuring peers;
- use neutral, encouraging prompts;
- offer a break or trusted-person route when appropriate;
- notice device, reading, sensory, language, or pacing barriers;
- stay within the approved profile and session goal.

## 11. Session Closing Pattern

A strong closing should normally include:

- invite participants to identify one practical next step / Hero Move if part of the approved profile;
- remind participants they do not need to share private reflection text;
- point to trusted-person or institutional supports if needed;
- collect only approved aggregate feedback;
- note implementation issues separately from private content;
- preview the next session if applicable.

## 12. Facilitator Implementation Notes

Permitted examples:

- “Session started 10 minutes late due to device distribution.”
- “Several participants requested larger text / more reading time.”
- “Network filtering blocked the initial route; IT resolved.”
- “Participants needed clarification that they did not have to share responses aloud.”
- “The 45-minute block was sufficient / insufficient for the approved sequence.”

Do not write:

- verbatim private reflection responses;
- a participant’s diagnosis or counseling history unless separately required in the institution’s proper system;
- speculative clinical interpretations;
- unnecessary personally identifying details;
- private disclosures into the Z-Girl scorecard.

## 13. Escalation Matrix

| Situation | Facilitator action |
|---|---|
| Technical problem | Use institutional / Z-Girl technical support route; do not send private reflection text in the ticket. |
| Accessibility barrier | Adjust within approved options; involve local accessibility/support owner when needed. |
| Participant wants to skip/stop | Respect the choice; offer approved alternative or trusted-person route. |
| Participant asks for clinical/medical advice | Explain role boundary; refer to appropriate qualified/local support. |
| Voluntary safeguarding disclosure | Follow institutional/legal process; do not investigate through Z-Girl. |
| Emergency / immediate danger | Use local emergency procedure / emergency services as appropriate. |
| Facilitator unsure whether material is approved | Pause adaptation and contact Z-Girl implementation lead. |

## 16. Reorientation triggers

A facilitator should receive an update or reorientation when:

- a new Z-Girl version materially changes facilitation;
- the facilitator begins using a new institutional profile;
- the audience or setting materially changes;
- material privacy/safeguarding guidance changes;
- repeated implementation problems indicate a need for reinforcement;
- the organization moves into Train-the-Trainer or broader licensing.
